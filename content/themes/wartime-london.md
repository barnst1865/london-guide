London was bombed for eight months straight in 1940 and 1941, then again by flying bombs and rockets in 1944 and 1945, and the city you walk around today is shaped by it: the gaps that became gardens, the churches rebuilt around a surviving apse, the housing estates where terraces used to be. This theme is about seeing that, not just reading about it.

The museums are the easy part, and they are excellent. The Churchill War Rooms under Whitehall and the Battle of Britain Bunker at Uxbridge are the two underground command posts, one where the war was run and one where the fighters were sent up; do both if you can. HMS *Belfast* is a cruiser you can climb through from bridge to boiler room. The Imperial War Museum, the National Army Museum and the RAF Museum are all free, all big, and all good with children if you pick the right galleries.

The harder, better part is out on the streets. The first V-1 fell on a railway bridge in Bow; the first V-2 on a quiet street in Chiswick; the last one on a block of flats in Stepney. Each has a plaque or a stone, and each is a few minutes from a station. Our [Blitz scars walk](trail:blitz-scars-walk) through the City goes from the firefighters' memorial by St Paul's to a ruined church turned garden by the Tower, past shrapnel-pocked walls and a Wren tower with traffic driving round it; the V&A's bombed colonnade in South Kensington and Cleopatra's Needle on the Embankment, scarred in 1917, are the same idea elsewhere.

Where a memorial stands among people's homes, as at Hughes Mansions or Staveley Road, go quietly. These are still neighborhoods, and in several cases the families still come.
