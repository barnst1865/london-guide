Americans and Brits share a language and misunderstand each other with great confidence. This is the short list of words that come up when you're getting around, eating and drinking, and going about your day. Where it helps, we link to the guide where the topic gets full treatment.

## Getting around

- **Tube / Underground:** London's underground train system, what you'd call the subway. "The Tube" is what everyone says; "the Underground" is the formal name.
- **Subway:** In London this means a pedestrian underpass, not a train. If someone points you to the subway, you'll be walking beneath a road.
- **Zone:** London's fare areas, numbered from 1 in the center outward. Your fare depends on the zones you travel through.
- **Tap in / tap out:** Touch your card or phone to the reader when you start and end a journey. Buses are tap in only. More in [getting around](guide:getting-around).
- **Fare cap:** The most you'll pay for a day (or a week) of travel on [TfL's system](https://tfl.gov.uk/fares/find-fares/capping), however many journeys you make. It applies automatically when you tap in and out.
- **Stand on the right:** On escalators, stand on the right and leave the left clear for people walking up or down.
- **Mind the gap:** The announcement about the space between the train and the platform. You'll hear it a lot.
- **Pavement:** The sidewalk. The "road" is where the cars are, so look right when you step off the curb.
- **Zebra crossing:** A pedestrian crossing marked with black and white stripes, where drivers are supposed to give way once you've stepped onto it. Look before you step.

## Food and drink

- **Full English:** The big cooked breakfast. Usually bacon, sausage, eggs, baked beans, tomato, mushrooms and toast, often with black pudding.
- **Black pudding:** A sausage made with pork blood and oatmeal, sliced and fried. It tastes like a rich, spiced sausage. Don't be thrown by the word "pudding"; this one is savory.
- **Bubble and squeak:** Leftover potato and cabbage (or other vegetables) mashed together and fried until crisp. The name is said to come from the sounds it makes in the pan.
- **Jellied eels:** An old East End dish. Chopped eel is boiled in a spiced stock that cools and sets into jelly, and it's served cold, usually with a splash of chili or malt vinegar or a shake of white pepper. It began in 18th-century East London as cheap food for the poor, because eels were plentiful in the Thames. The European eel is now critically endangered, and only a few old shops still serve the dish. Try it if you're curious; it's an acquired taste.
- **Pie and mash:** Another East End classic. A minced-beef pie with mashed potato and a parsley sauce called **liquor**. Liquor has nothing to do with alcohol; traditionally it was made with the water the eels had been stewed in, though many shops now use chicken stock. The dish and the jellied eels came from the same shops, and some still serve them side by side.
- **Yorkshire pudding:** Not a dessert. It's a baked, puffed-up batter, crisp outside and soft inside, served with roast beef and gravy. See [Sunday roasts](guide:eating-out).
- **Pudding / afters:** "Pudding" is what Brits call dessert, and "afters" is the casual word for the same course.- **Starter / main:** A starter is an appetizer and the main is the main course. "Entrée" is sometimes used for the starter on British menus, so check before you order.
- **Crisps / chips:** Crisps are what you'd call potato chips. Chips are thick-cut fries, as in fish and chips. What Americans call fries may be listed as fries, or as thin chips.
- **Squash:** A concentrated fruit syrup that you dilute with water. "Orange squash" is a common kids' drink.
- **Shandy:** Beer mixed with lemonade. The lemonade is a clear fizzy lemon soda, not the still kind you'd make at home.
- **Bitter:** A style of beer, usually amber or brown and served from a cask. Despite the name it isn't especially bitter. It's served cooler than room temperature but not ice cold, and with gentle carbonation. That's normal.
- **Cider:** Alcoholic. If you want the non-alcoholic kind, ask for apple juice.
- **Pint:** About 568 milliliters, which is bigger than the US pint. A half is a half pint. More in [pub etiquette](guide:pub-etiquette).

## Everyday life

- **Toilet / loo:** The restroom. Ask for "the toilet" or "the loo." Asking for the bathroom may get you a bath.
- **Lift:** The elevator. The ground floor is level with the street, and the first floor is one flight up.
- **Cheers / ta:** Both can mean "thanks." Cheers is also what you say when you clink glasses, and "ta" is the quicker, friendlier version.
- **You alright?:** A greeting, not a question about your health. The correct answer is "Yeah, you?" or just "Alright."
- **Queue:** Both a noun and a verb. A line is a queue, and Brits take queuing seriously.
- **Quid / fiver / tenner:** A quid is a pound (£1), a fiver is £5, and a tenner is £10.
- **Bank holiday:** A public holiday, often on a Monday. Check opening hours and train times before you head out.
- **Takeaway:** Takeout, or the shop that sells it.
- **Chemist:** A pharmacy.
- **Fortnight:** Two weeks.
- **Pants / trousers / jumper / trainers:** Pants are underwear, trousers are what you'd call pants, a jumper is a sweater, and trainers are sneakers.
- **The bill:** The check. It usually arrives only when you ask for it. More in [eating out](guide:eating-out).
