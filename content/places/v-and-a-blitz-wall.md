Walk along Exhibition Road toward the V&A's side entrance and look at the stone columns of the screen along the pavement. They are riddled with craters, some the size of a fist. Two big bombs fell in the road during the Blitz, wrecking the west side of the museum and blowing in its doors, and the Portland stone took the shrapnel. The museum decided in the 1980s not to repair it and asked David Kindersley, the great letter-cutter, to carve an explanation into the damaged stone itself: "The damage to these walls is the result of enemy bombing during the Blitz of the Second World War 1939–1945 and is left as a memorial to the enduring values of this great museum in a time of conflict."

When the architect Amanda Levete remodeled this entrance in 2017 she kept every scarred column, took out the infill wall between them to make a new courtyard, and hung aluminum gates perforated in a pattern copied from the shrapnel marks. The damage became the design.

It is on the public street, so you can see it any time, and the museum behind it is free and one of the great museums of the world; this is just the angle most visitors walk straight past.

**Our tip:** Come up from South Kensington through the pedestrian tunnel, which puts you right there. The [Brompton Oratory](place:brompton-oratory), with its Cold War story, is a block away.
