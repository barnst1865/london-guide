The Post Office Tower, as it was, went up in 1964 to carry microwave links out of London, and Harold Wilson made the first call from it in October 1965. For fifteen years it had public viewing galleries and a Butlins revolving restaurant that turned once every 22 minutes. Then a bomb went off in the restaurant's toilets early on 31 October 1971; the Angry Brigade claimed it, the IRA denied it, nobody was ever charged, and the public never went back up.

Here is the spy part. For decades the tower was officially a secret, a node in a government microwave network called Backbone that had been planned with nuclear war in mind, and it was supposedly illegal to photograph the tallest building in London. In February 1993 the MP Kate Hoey stood up in the Commons and gave its address, 60 Cleveland Street, which rather ended the pretense. The claim that it was left off Ordnance Survey maps is a myth (it is on them from 1971), and so is the idea that the tower was built to survive a nuclear blast: the slim cylinder was about keeping the dishes steady in a gale.

BT sold it in 2024 and a hotel conversion is planned, with public floors near the top promised for the 2030s. Until then, look at it from Fitzroy Square.
