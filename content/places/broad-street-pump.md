In the last days of August 1854 cholera tore through the streets around what was then Broad Street, killing more than six hundred people in a few weeks. Everyone knew it was spread by bad air. A local doctor, John Snow, didn't believe it. He plotted every death on a street map, saw them cluster around one public water pump, interviewed the survivors, and on 8 September persuaded the parish to take the handle off. The outbreak was already fading, but the method stuck: the dot map and the removed handle are the founding gestures of epidemiology and public health, which is why this is a place of pilgrimage for doctors and data people from all over the world. A curate later found the cause: a cesspit three feet from the well, leaking from a house where a baby had died of cholera.

The replica pump, handle-less of course, was first put up in 1992 on the wrong corner. It came down in 2015 and was reinstated in 2018 on the real spot, on the kerb outside the side door of the pub that was renamed the John Snow in 1954. Look for the pink granite kerbstone beside the plinth: that marks the original pump.

**Our tip:** A five-minute stop. The pub is a Samuel Smith's, so cheap beer and no phones at the bar; Snow himself was a teetotaler, which the regulars find funny.
