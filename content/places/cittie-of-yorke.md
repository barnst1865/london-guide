Walk through the front bar to the back and you'll find one of London's most dramatic pub rooms: a long, high hall with an open timber roof, a gallery of huge wine casks, and a row of small wooden booths along one side, built for private conversations. In the middle stands an odd triangular iron stove, believed to have come from nearby Gray's Inn around 1815.

It looks medieval, but most of it was built in 1923–24 for the wine merchants Henekey's, in a deliberately old-fashioned style. The "1430" on the front refers to the claim that there has been a pub on this site since then, which nobody has proved. Dylan Thomas scribbled a poem here in 1951, when it was still Henekey's, and it came to light in 2014.

It's a Samuel Smith's pub, so the beer is cheap and the brewery's house rules apply.

**Our tip:** Grab one of the booths in the back hall. Sam Smith's rules: no phones or laptops, and no swearing.
