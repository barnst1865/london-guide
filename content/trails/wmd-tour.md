Weapons of mass destruction are usually talked about in the abstract, as treaties, stockpiles and acronyms. In London they have street addresses. Over the last two centuries the city has been a target, a laboratory, a command post and a crime scene, and every category of weapon of mass destruction has left a mark here that you can walk between in a day. Nuclear: the bunkers under Whitehall, and a reactor that hummed under Greenwich for decades. Radiological: the polonium that killed Alexander Litvinenko in a Mayfair hotel bar. Chemical: the London hotel where the Salisbury attackers slept. Biological: the ricin pellet in Georgi Markov's thigh on Waterloo Bridge, and, at the hopeful end of the same science, the water pump where John Snow worked out how disease actually spreads. Missiles: the first V-1 and V-2 to hit the city.

Two chapters of the story are outside London. In Salisbury, ninety minutes from Waterloo, Sergei and Yulia Skripal were found on a bench in the Maltings in March 2018 after a nerve agent was smeared on their front door; the bench is gone, the pub and the restaurant have reopened, and the house is a private home again. In Amesbury, a few miles away, Dawn Sturgess died that July after her partner gave her a discarded bottle of what he thought was perfume. The public inquiry into her death concluded in December 2025 that the attack was authorized at the top of the Russian state. We do not pin her home or the Skripals'; we do recommend the cathedral. And at Aldermaston in Berkshire the Atomic Weapons Establishment has built Britain's warheads since 1950 and is firmly closed, though the Easter marches that started there in 1958 are a story in themselves. Both will get day-trip pages in time.

A word on tone. The tour has a dark comedy to it, and we enjoy that. But it runs through places where real people died, some of them bystanders, and several stops are working hotels, churches and homes. Read the plaques, take the photographs of buildings not people, and keep the jokes for the pub afterward.

Start early, book the War Rooms in advance, and wear shoes you can walk three miles in.
